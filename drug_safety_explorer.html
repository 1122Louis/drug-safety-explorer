Reports per 100,000 prescriptions — rough estimate

Raw report counts are unfair to compare: a drug used by millions will naturally have more reports. Dividing by prescription volume is a fairer way to compare. Here we use Medicare Part D claims for 2020–2024 as the denominator.

⚠️ Read this as a rough signal, not a real risk rate. The reports come from everyone (all ages, worldwide), but the prescription count is Medicare only (mostly 65+, U.S.) — so the two don't perfectly match. It's most meaningful for comparing two similar drugs, not as an absolute number. Drugs with no reliable Medicare data (over-the-counter or newly-covered) show no rate.

Who is affected & how serious

The age and sex of people in these reports, and how many describe a serious outcome. Many reports leave these blank, so percentages are of the reports that filled them in.

⬆ Signal detection — reported unusually often

A side effect's PRR (Proportional Reporting Ratio) compares how often it's reported for this drug versus for all drugs. PRR ≈ 1 is ordinary; a high PRR means it stands out — a signal worth investigating. This is the real method regulators use.

⚠️ A signal is a question, not an answer. A high PRR means a side effect is reported more than expected — it does not prove the drug causes it. Reporting is biased by media attention, lawsuits, and how new a drug is. Real safety decisions need clinical studies. Highlighted rows meet the standard screening rule (PRR ≥ 2, at least 3 reports, and statistically notable).

📈 Emerging signals — reported more over time

A side effect can be common but steady, or newly rising. This tracks each side effect's share of the drug's reports year by year — using share (not raw counts) so a drug simply becoming more popular doesn't look like a new problem. Rising share = an emerging concern.

⚠️ A rising share reflects rising reporting, not proven rising risk. Lawsuits, news coverage, and new warnings all drive reports up. It flags what people are increasingly reporting — a lead to investigate, not a measured change in how dangerous the drug is.

💊 Commonly taken alongside

Other drugs (by active ingredient) most often listed in the same reports — a picture of what else these patients are on. Shown as a share of the drug's reports; the drug itself is excluded.

⚠️ Taken together ≠ an interaction. This mostly reflects the typical treatment regimen for these patients (e.g. other diabetes or heart drugs), not proof that the combination caused harm. A report listing several drugs doesn't say which one is responsible.

🗂️ Reporting details — who, where & how it's used

Who submitted these reports, which countries they came from, and how often the drug was being used off-label (for something it isn't officially approved for). Shares are of the drug's total reports.

⚠️ Most reports come from consumers and from the U.S., which shapes what gets reported — this is a picture of who reports, not of where the drug is most risky. Off-label use is common and often legal; it's noted here because it affects how reports should be read.

Drugs most associated with this side effect

Among reports mentioning this side effect, which drugs show up disproportionately? Ranked by PRR — how much more often the drug is linked to this effect than expected. Grouped by active ingredient; only drugs with at least 10 such reports are shown.

⚠️ This shows reporting patterns, not proven cause. A drug ranking high may simply be widely used, newly launched, or in the news. A high PRR is a reason to investigate, never a conclusion. Reports come from patients and doctors and are not verified.
